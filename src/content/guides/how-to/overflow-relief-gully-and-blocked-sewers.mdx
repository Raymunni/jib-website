Somewhere outside your house there's probably a small round grate that nobody ever thinks about. It's an overflow relief gully, and it's there for one unpleasant moment: when the sewer backs up, it makes the mess happen in the garden rather than on your bathroom floor. Here's how to find it, keep it working, and who pays if it ever overflows.

**If wastewater is overflowing right now:** keep people and pets away from it, stop using your toilets, taps and appliances, and call your water authority's 24-hour faults line. The main numbers are further down.

## What an overflow relief gully does

Wastewater from your house runs through a private pipe into the sewer main. If that line blocks, the wastewater has to go somewhere. Without a relief point, it comes up through the lowest fittings in the house, which are usually the shower and the toilet.

The gully is a deliberate weak point. South East Water says it should sit **at least 150 mm lower than the lowest fitting in the house** and **75 mm above the surrounding ground**, so wastewater escapes there first, and rain and garden dirt stay out.

## How to find yours

Look for a small round grate, often 100 mm or so across, set in a concrete collar:

- near an outside tap or the kitchen or laundry wall
- beside a bathroom on the outside of the house
- under a tap with a small drain below it

The grate should be loose, because it needs to lift under pressure. Some homes have more than one, and some apartment buildings have them in shared areas.

## Keep it working: the twice-yearly check

This takes about two minutes, and it's all DIY:

1. **Find it and clear it.** Pull back mulch, soil, pot plants, pavers and anything else sitting on or around it.
2. **Check the height.** The rim should sit above the ground around it, so rain doesn't run in. New garden beds and paving are the usual reason it ends up buried.
3. **Check the grate lifts freely.** Don't glue, screw or cement it down.
4. **Check it isn't full.** If you can see wastewater or smell sewage, call a plumber.

If it's lower than the ground around it, or you've done new paving over the area, get a licensed plumber to assess it. Raising or moving a gully is licensed plumbing work.

## Signs of a blockage

Sydney Water lists these as signs of a wastewater blockage:

- Sinks, baths or toilets emptying slowly
- A toilet that isn't flushing properly, or has overflowed
- Gurgling noises from a toilet or floor drain
- Wastewater overflowing from the gully near your outside tap

One slow fixture usually means a blockage just in that fixture, which you can often clear yourself. See [how to unblock a sink, shower or toilet](/guides/how-to/how-to-unblock-a-drain/). **Several fixtures at once, or the gully overflowing, usually means the main line.** Stop using water and make a call.

## Who pays for a blocked sewer?

The general rule across Australian water authorities is this: **the authority looks after its sewer main, and you look after the private pipes on your side.** Exactly where that line falls depends on your authority.

| Where the blockage is | Who usually fixes it |
|---|---|
| In the sewer main, normally outside the property | The water authority |
| In your own pipes, called property plumbing | You, using a licensed plumber |
| Not sure | Call the authority or a licensed plumber |

**Don't pay first and hope.** A few details can catch people out:

- **Call the authority before a plumber if the sewer may be the problem.** Plumbers can't work on the authority's pipes. SA Water asks plumbers to call it when they find a fault in its infrastructure.
- **Claiming back a plumber's bill has conditions.** South East Water says it may refuse to cover the cost if the plumber didn't refer the blockage to it, or if work such as high-pressure jetting or root clearing was done. Ask your authority before the work starts.
- **Renting?** Tell your landlord or rental provider straight away. Your lease or agreement may name the plumber to use.

## Why sewers block

- **Wet wipes, even "flushable" ones.** Tissues, paper towels and wipes don't break down like toilet paper.
- **Fat poured down the sink**, which sets hard further along the pipe.
- **Tree roots.** Sydney Water says roots can enter small cracks in wastewater pipes looking for nutrients. If yours keep coming back, talk to your authority and consider what's planted over the line.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the small grate outside my house?**
It's probably an overflow relief gully. It lets wastewater escape outside if the sewer line blocks, rather than coming up inside your home.

**Can I cover my overflow relief gully?**
No. Keep it clear and above the surrounding ground, and never glue or cement down the grate. Covering it can mean a sewage overflow ends up inside the house.

**Why does my gully smell?**
Gullies usually hold some water, which stops sewer gases coming through. If it dries out, adding water from a nearby tap can help. If it smells of sewage, or there's wastewater in it, call a licensed plumber.

**Who pays if a tree root blocks my sewer?**
It depends on where the blockage is and whose tree and pipe are involved. Roots in your own pipes are usually yours to fix. Call your water authority first if you're not sure.

**Can I clear a sewer blockage myself?**
You can clear a simple blockage in one fixture. Clearing a main line needs a licensed plumber, and overflows should go to your water authority's faults line.
