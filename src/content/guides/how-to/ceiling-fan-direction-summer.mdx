If your ceiling fan has been running all summer and the room still feels stuffy, there's a good chance it's spinning the wrong way. It takes 10 seconds to check and 30 seconds to fix.

## Summer: anticlockwise, air down

For most ceiling fans, **summer mode is anticlockwise** when you're standing underneath looking up. The angle of the blades pushes air **down** onto the room, and that moving air over your skin is what makes you feel cooler.

According to energy.gov.au, fans can make it feel about **3°C cooler** and cost only around **2 cents an hour** to run. The catch: a fan doesn't cool the room itself, it cools *you*. It has to be blowing on you to work, so turn it off when you leave the room.

## Winter: clockwise, on low

In winter, flip it to **clockwise on a low speed**. The fan gently pulls air up and pushes the warm air that collects near the ceiling out and down the walls, without creating a cold draught. It's most useful in rooms with high ceilings or a heater running.

## The 10-second test

Blade angles differ between brands, so trust the feel, not the arrow:

1. Turn the fan to its **highest speed**.
2. Stand directly underneath it.
3. **Strong breeze on your face?** Summer mode. **Barely anything?** It's pushing air up, which is winter mode.

## How to change the direction

**Always turn the fan off first and wait until the blades stop completely.** Changing direction while it's spinning can damage the motor.

- **Slide switch on the motor housing.** Most older and AC-motor fans have a small switch on the side of the round housing above the blades. Stand on a sturdy step ladder, flick it, and turn the fan back on.
- **Remote control.** Many newer fans, especially DC-motor fans, have a reverse button (often marked with circular arrows) on the remote instead of a switch on the fan.
- **Wall controller.** Some wall controllers have a forward/reverse button.

If you can't find any of these, look up the model in the manual or on the maker's website.

## "Do fans spin the other way in Australia?"

No. A ceiling fan pushes air down or up because of the angle of its blades, and that has nothing to do with the hemisphere. The idea comes from the myth that water drains the other way down south. Whatever the fan's label or advice from an overseas website says, the summer setting is the one where you feel the breeze standing underneath.

## While you're up there: two more quick jobs

**Dust the blades.** Slide an old pillowcase over each blade and pull it back, so the dust falls inside the pillowcase instead of onto you and the couch.

**Fix a wobble.** Turn the fan off and check the screws that hold each blade to its bracket, and the bracket to the motor. A loose screw is the usual cause. If it still wobbles, a cheap balancing kit from the hardware store fixes most fans.

**If the fan hums, smells of burning, sparks, or the light flickers, switch it off at the wall and call a licensed electrician.** Anything involving the wiring, or installing or replacing a fan, is licensed electrical work in every Australian state. ## Fans or aircon?

Use both. energy.gov.au says each degree of extra cooling increases your aircon's energy use by **5% to 10%**, so running a fan with the aircon set a couple of degrees higher can feel just as comfortable for less. ## Frequently asked questions

**Which way should a ceiling fan turn in summer in Australia?**
Anticlockwise for most fans, looking up. It pushes air down onto you. Check by standing underneath on high: you should feel a strong breeze.

**Is ceiling fan direction different in Australia?**
No. The direction depends on the blade angle, not the hemisphere.

**Where is the reverse switch on a ceiling fan?**
Usually a small slide switch on the side of the motor housing. On many DC fans, it's a button on the remote instead.

**Should I leave the ceiling fan on all day?**
Only when someone's in the room. Fans cool people, not rooms, so a fan in an empty room just uses power.
