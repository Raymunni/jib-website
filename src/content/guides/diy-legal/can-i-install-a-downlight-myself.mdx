Downlights look like the easiest electrical job in the house: a hole, two spring clips and a plug-in connector. That's why so many people search for whether they can do it themselves. The answer is no, but the reason is more interesting than "because the rules say so", and there's a fair bit of the job you can still own.

This guide is about downlights specifically: ratings, insulation, halogens and holes. For the general rule on fans, pendants and other light fittings, see [can I install a ceiling fan or light fitting myself?](/guides/diy-legal/can-i-install-a-ceiling-fan-or-light-fitting-myself/)

## Can I install or replace a downlight myself?

No. Installing, replacing, moving or removing a downlight fitting is electrical work, and it needs a licensed electrician in every state and territory. That's true even when the new fitting has a plug-in lead and the old one has a matching socket in the ceiling.

Queensland's Electrical Safety Office defines electrical work to include installing, altering, removing or replacing electrical equipment, and lists "replacing a batten holder with a new light fitting" as licensed work. Energy Safe Victoria's line is that electrical work "isn't a DIY job, even for small jobs". In Queensland, DIY electrical work carries penalties of up to $40,000 for an individual, and the regulator warns your insurer may refuse a claim for a fire caused by illegal electrical work.

## What about changing the globe?

That's yours. Changing a globe or lamp is normal use of the fitting. Switch the light off first and let a halogen globe cool before you touch it, because they run very hot.

Swapping a halogen globe for an LED globe that fits the same socket is still just changing a globe. Where it stops being yours:

- **The old fitting has a transformer** (common with 12 V halogen downlights) and the LED globe flickers, buzzes or won't work with it. Replacing the transformer or driver is electrical work.
- **The fitting itself is cracked, scorched or loose** in the ceiling. Leave it off and call an electrician.
- **You want a different style of downlight**, such as an integrated LED unit with no separate globe. That's a new fitting.

## Why are halogen downlights a fire risk?

Because they get extremely hot and sit in a roof space full of things that burn. Fire and Rescue NSW says halogen globes can reach up to 370°C, and that the heat can cause ceiling insulation to smoulder or ignite if it covers the light or its transformer. It also warns never to install them near structural timber, and to keep leaves and dust from building up around them in the roof.

The Australian Government's YourHome guide is direct: older incandescent and halogen downlights cannot be covered with insulation, and "should be removed and replaced with IC-4 rated LEDs". Mains voltage halogen globes are also being phased out where an LED equivalent is available.

**If you find insulation lying over a halogen downlight**, turn that light off and leave it off until an electrician has looked at it. Don't pull insulation around in the roof yourself while the lights are on.

## What does IC-4 mean?

IC stands for insulation contact. YourHome describes an IC rated downlight as one that "can be abutted and directly covered with insulation", so the ceiling insulation can run unbroken over the top. That's safer, and it stops the heat loss you get from a gap around every light.

- **IC-4 rated LEDs** are what YourHome recommends as the simplest choice. Most LED downlights now sold in Australia are IC rated, but check the box and the fitting.
- **Some LED fittings can't be covered.** They need a gap, or a fire safety barrier tested to AS/NZS 5110. Look for a "do not cover" symbol in the instructions.
- **Older fittings need clearances.** A WorkSafe Queensland safety alert, citing the Wiring Rules of the time, gave default clearances of 50 mm from incandescent downlights and 200 mm from halogen downlights, plus 50 mm around any transformer, unless the fitting is designed to be covered or sits in a suitable fire-resistant enclosure. Your electrician works to the current Wiring Rules and the fitting's instructions.
- **Downlight covers and barriers** used to hold loose-fill insulation back must be fire-resistant, secured in position and meet AS 60598.1 and 60598.2, according to Fire and Rescue NSW. Dropping a cheap cover over an old halogen isn't the same thing.

## Getting ceiling insulation installed?

Tell the installer about every downlight before they start, especially halogens and anything with a transformer. South Australia's guidance lists halogen and extra low voltage downlights among the things that need safe distances from insulation, and says the installer should check whether an electrician is required. This is often the best time to have old halogens replaced with IC-4 LEDs, so the insulation can go over the top.

## What you can do yourself, and when to call a licensed electrician

**Yours to do:**

- **Change globes**, including like-for-like LED globes, with the light switched off and cool.
- **Choose the fittings.** IC-4 rated, the right colour temperature for the room, dimmable if you have or want a dimmer (and compatible with it), and a suitable IP rating for bathrooms or outdoor eaves.
- **Match the cut-out size.** Pick new fittings with the same cut-out as the old holes, or ask the electrician about adaptor plates, so you're not left with gaps.
- **Plan the layout.** YourHome suggests avoiding downlights for general lighting, as up to six may be needed to light the same area as one pendant. Mark where each light should go and how it's switched before the visit.
- **Patch and paint** old holes once the electrician has removed the fitting and made the cable safe. Our [plasterboard patching guide](/guides/how-to/how-to-patch-a-hole-in-plasterboard/) covers it. In a pre-1990 home, the ceiling sheeting could contain asbestos, so have it tested before cutting or sanding.
- **Go plug-in** where you just need more light: floor and table lamps, plug-in LED strips and rechargeable stick-on lights don't touch the fixed wiring.

**Call a licensed electrician for:**

- Installing new downlights, adding more, or moving any.
- Replacing a downlight fitting, transformer or driver.
- An LED globe that flickers or buzzes in an old fitting.
- Discoloured or scorched ceiling around a light, a burning smell, or a fitting that's hot to be near.
- Insulation found touching or covering a halogen downlight.

## Live in a strata building?

In NSW, "changes to recessed light fittings" are listed as a minor renovation in strata. You need permission from the owners corporation before the work, approved by a majority vote at a strata meeting. Other states have their own strata rules, so check your by-laws before booking the electrician.

## After the job

Ask the electrician for the compliance certificate your state uses and keep it with your home records. The [power point guide](/guides/diy-legal/can-i-replace-a-power-point-myself/) lists the certificate name in each state.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I replace halogen downlights with LED myself?**
You can swap a halogen globe for an LED globe that fits the same socket. Replacing the fitting, or the transformer behind it, is a job for a licensed electrician.

**Can insulation touch LED downlights?**
Only if the fitting is IC rated for it. Check the rating and the instructions. Some LEDs need a clearance or a tested barrier, and old halogen and incandescent fittings must never be covered.

**Can a handyman install downlights?**
Only if they hold an electrical licence. A handyman without one can't legally do it, however simple the fitting looks.

**Are downlight covers a fix for old halogens?**
Fire and Rescue NSW says a barrier or cover used to keep loose insulation away from a downlight must be made of fire-resistant material, secured in place and meet AS 60598.1 and 60598.2. Replacing the halogens with IC-4 LEDs is the simpler and safer fix, and it's what YourHome recommends.

**Why does my new LED downlight flicker?**
Often it's a mismatch with an old dimmer or transformer. Don't keep swapping parts behind the ceiling. Note which lights flicker and when, and let the electrician sort the driver or dimmer.
