Swapping a toilet looks like a bolt-it-down job, which is why it's one of the most common DIY plumbing questions. A lot of the answers online come from overseas, where the rules are different. Here's the Australian answer.

## The rule in NSW

In NSW you need a licence "before you can do any plumbing, draining or gasfitting work… regardless of the cost". The owner exemptions cover repairing a tap or showerhead, not replacing a toilet. Individuals doing unlicensed plumbing work face fines of up to $22,000.

## The rule everywhere else

Replacing a whole toilet suite is licensed plumbing work in every state and territory. It involves the water connection to the cistern and the connection to the sewer or drain — both regulated work.

## What you *can* fix inside the cistern

A running or slow-filling toilet is usually a worn washer or seal in the cistern. Some states let you replace those parts yourself:

| State | DIY-OK cistern repairs |
|---|---|
| Queensland | Drop-valve washer, float-valve washer, suction-cup rubber |
| Western Australia | Cistern washer |
| Other states | Treat cistern repairs as licensed work unless your regulator confirms otherwise |

## Jobs around a toilet swap you can do

- **Choose the suite:** check the "set-out" (distance from the wall to the centre of the waste pipe) so the new pan actually fits your existing outlet. Measuring this before you buy avoids a costly return.
- **Check the water rating:** WaterMark certification and the WELS star rating are on the box.
- **Prep the room:** clear it, protect the floor, and plan any tiling or painting for after the plumber has finished.
- **Remove old accessories** like the toilet-roll holder or towel rail.

## Signs it's more than a washer

Water pooling around the base, a pan that rocks, a sewer smell or a crack in the pan or cistern all point to a problem with the seal or the fixture itself. That's a plumber's job — and worth doing quickly, since a leaking pan seal can damage the floor.
