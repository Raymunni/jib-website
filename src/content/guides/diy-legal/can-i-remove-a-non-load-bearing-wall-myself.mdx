Knocking out a wall between the kitchen and the living room is one of the most satisfying renovations there is. It's also one where a wrong guess costs the most. The question isn't really "can I swing a hammer", it's three questions: is the wall holding anything up, what's inside it, and what is it made of? Get those answered first and the rest is often yours to do.

**If you're not sure, don't start.** Cracks in the ceiling, doors that stop closing or a sagging roof line can all follow the removal of a wall that turned out to be doing a job.

## How do I tell if a wall is load bearing?

You can't be sure by looking, so have a structural engineer or registered building surveyor check it before you touch it. What you can do is gather the clues they'll ask about, which makes the visit quicker:

- **External walls** are load bearing. This guide is only about internal walls.
- **Look in the roof space** (safely, on the joists, with a torch). A wall is more likely to be load bearing if the ceiling joists sit on it or are joined over it, or if roof struts or a beam come down onto its top plate.
- **Direction matters, but isn't proof.** Walls running across the ceiling joists are more often load bearing than walls running the same way, but there are plenty of exceptions.
- **Check what's above and below.** A wall with another wall, a beam or a post directly above it on a second storey, or with piers or a strip footing under it in the subfloor, is likely carrying load.
- **Bracing walls** may carry no roof weight at all but still stop the house racking in the wind. They're often lined with sheet bracing behind the plasterboard, and only a professional can tell you whether one can go.

Victoria's building rules capture the test well: the exemption from a permit only covers work that doesn't include "the removal or alteration of any element of the building that is contributing to the support of any other element of the building". If an engineer says the wall is doing that, you need a beam designed for it and a building approval.

## Do I need approval to remove an internal wall?

A load-bearing wall needs building approval in every state. A genuinely non-load-bearing wall often doesn't, but the rules differ:

| State | Non-load-bearing internal wall | Load-bearing wall |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | Minor internal alterations can be exempt development, as long as the work doesn't alter the load-bearing capacity of the building and meets the building code | Needs a complying development certificate or a development application |
| VIC | Alterations are exempt from a building permit if they don't affect structural soundness, don't remove anything supporting another part of the building, and aren't on a Heritage Register building | Building permit from a registered building surveyor |
| QLD | Councils such as Logan say a private building certifier can confirm whether approval is needed | Logan lists "removal or alteration load bearing walls" as needing building approval |
| WA, SA, TAS, ACT, NT | Check with your council or a building surveyor | Assume approval is needed |

Two things catch people out:

- **Heritage.** Heritage-listed homes and some heritage areas have extra controls, even for internal work in some cases. Ask your council first.
- **Strata.** In NSW, "changes to internal walls" are a minor renovation in a strata building. You need permission from the owners corporation, approved by a majority vote at a strata meeting, before you start. Other states have their own strata rules, so check your by-laws.

## What's inside the wall?

Most internal walls carry something. Before any demolition, find out what, because each part belongs to a licensed trade:

- **Power points, switches and cables:** disconnecting, removing or moving them is electrical work, which needs a licensed electrician in every state. Queensland's regulator names "altering the location of an existing power point" as licensed work, and Energy Safe Victoria says electrical work isn't DIY "even for small jobs". The electrician will make the cables safe or reroute them, and that has to happen before you cut.
- **Water and waste pipes:** common in walls behind kitchens, bathrooms and laundries. Capping or rerouting them is plumbing work for a licensed plumber. In NSW, a licence is needed for any plumbing work regardless of cost.
- **Gas pipes:** licensed gasfitter only.
- **Data, TV and alarm cables:** have them moved before you start, so you're not guessing which cut is safe.

Queensland's Electrical Safety Office also warns about wiring hidden in wall cavities when cutting holes or driving screws or nails into walls. A stud finder with a live wire setting helps, but it isn't a guarantee. Switching off at the switchboard and having the electrician confirm the wall is clear is the safe way.

## Could the wall contain asbestos?

Yes, if your home was built or renovated before 1990. The national asbestos agency says a house of that age "probably has some asbestos", and lists wall and ceiling sheeting, particularly in kitchens, bathrooms, toilets and laundries, and the backing for wall tiles and splashbacks, as common places to find it.

- **You can't tell by looking.** Have a sample tested, or treat the lining as asbestos until a test says otherwise.
- **Don't drill, cut, sand or smash it** to find out. High-speed power tools are exactly what the agency says not to use on asbestos material.
- **Removal rules differ by state.** A tradie can't remove it unless they're licensed, or the job is under 10 m² of non-friable (bonded) asbestos and they're trained for it. 10 m² is about one wall of an average bathroom. In the ACT, homeowners can't remove any amount themselves. The agency recommends using a professional, and says it's often cheaper than people expect.
- **Disposal is regulated.** Asbestos waste must go to a landfill licensed to take it. Putting it in your household bin can mean a fine.

If the lining is plasterboard on timber studs and the test is clear, the demolition is much simpler.

## What you can do yourself, and when to call a licensed trade

Once the wall is confirmed non-load-bearing, any approval is sorted, the services are disconnected and the asbestos test is clear, a lot of the job is usually yours.

**Usually yours to do:**

- Clear the room, cover the floor and seal doorways with plastic to keep dust in.
- Remove skirting, architraves and cornice along the wall.
- Take off the plasterboard linings and pull out the timber studs and plates.
- Patch the ceiling and walls where the old wall met them. Our [plasterboard patching guide](/guides/how-to/how-to-patch-a-hole-in-plasterboard/) covers the small holes.
- Fill the gap in the flooring. There's often no floor covering under an old wall, so plan for it.
- Paint, and put new skirting and cornice in.

**Call a licensed or registered professional for:**

- **Deciding whether the wall is load bearing:** a structural engineer or building surveyor.
- **Any approval:** a building surveyor or certifier, or your council.
- **Power points, switches, lights and cables in the wall:** a licensed electrician.
- **Water, waste or gas pipes:** a licensed plumber or gasfitter.
- **Testing or removing suspected asbestos:** an asbestos assessor and a licensed removalist.
- **A replacement beam** if the wall turns out to be load bearing: engineer-designed, built under approval.

## Owner-builder thresholds, if it's part of a bigger renovation

Taking out one wall rarely reaches these limits. A whole kitchen and living renovation can, and above them you need an owner-builder permit to manage the building work yourself:

- **NSW:** renovation work valued at over $10,000 needs an owner-builder permit, and over $20,000 you also need to complete certain units of competency.
- **Queensland:** a permit is needed for a building project valued at more than $11,000. The QBCC says owner-builders still can't do occupational work such as plumbing, draining or gasfitting without the matching licence.
- **Victoria:** a certificate of consent is needed before carrying out domestic building work over $16,000.
- **Western Australia:** owner-builder approval may be needed where a building permit is required and the work is worth more than $20,000.
- **SA, Tasmania, ACT and NT:** check with your state's building regulator.

An owner-builder permit is about managing building work. It never lets you do your own electrical work, and in Queensland the QBCC is clear that plumbing and gasfitting still need the licence.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I remove a stud wall without council approval?**
Often, if it's confirmed non-load-bearing and your state's exemption applies, as in the NSW and Victorian rules above. Confirm it with a building surveyor or your council first, especially for a heritage-listed home or a strata unit.

**How much of a wall can I remove before it needs an engineer?**
There's no safe size. Even a short section of a load-bearing wall needs a properly designed beam. The question is what the wall does, not how much of it goes.

**Can I just cut the plasterboard to look inside?**
Only once you're sure it isn't asbestos sheeting and there's no wiring where you're cutting. In a pre-1990 home, have the lining tested first, and switch the power off at the switchboard before cutting.

**Who moves the power point that's on the wall?**
A licensed electrician. Removing or relocating a power point is licensed electrical work everywhere in Australia, and it needs doing before the wall comes down.

**What happens if I remove a load-bearing wall without approval?**
Beyond the structural risk, unapproved building work can cause problems with your council, your insurer and a future sale. See [what happens if you build without council approval](/guides/council-approval/what-happens-if-you-build-without-council-approval/).
