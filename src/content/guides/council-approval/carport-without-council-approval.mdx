Carport kits are sold like garden furniture, flat packed and delivered to the driveway. The approval rules are much stricter than the packaging suggests: in Queensland, Victoria and WA, nearly every carport big enough for a car needs approval.

## Limits by state

The short answer: NSW is the only state where a normal single carport is commonly exempt.

| State | No approval needed if… |
|---|---|
| QLD | No more than 10 m² in plan area, 2.4 m high (mean height 2.1 m), no side longer than 5 m, and not in Wind Region C (tropical cyclone area). Bigger carports need building approval |
| NSW | Up to 20 m² (lots of 300 m² or less), 25 m² (larger lots in most zones) or 50 m² (larger lots in rural zones and R5); no more than 3 m high; at least 1 m behind the building line; 900 mm from boundaries (5 m in zones RU1 to RU4, RU6 and R5); at least 2 sides and a third of the perimeter open |
| VIC | Never. Every carport needs a building permit |
| WA | Freestanding, 10 m² or less, no more than 2.4 m high within 1 m of a boundary (3 m if further away), not masonry, and no closer to the street than the house. Attached carports need a permit |
| SA, TAS, ACT, NT | Check with your council. We couldn't confirm their limits from an official source |

## Queensland: plan on building approval

In Queensland a carport is a class 10 structure. Under schedule 1 of the Building Regulation 2021, it needs no building approval only if it's **no more than 10 m²** in plan area (overhangs count), **no more than 2.4 m high**, has **no side longer than 5 m**, and isn't in Wind Region C. That's at most 5 m by 2 m, which is too tight for most family cars. Brisbane City Council puts it plainly: if you're building a carport on a property with an existing house, you'll need building approval, arranged through a licensed building certifier.

Planning approval is a separate question. In Brisbane, a carport is generally accepted development (no planning approval) in a residential zone without a relevant overlay or neighbourhood plan, provided it meets the Dwelling house code.

### Where a QLD carport can go

The siting rules come from the Queensland Development Code, MP 1.1 for lots under 450 m² and MP 1.2 for lots of 450 m² and over. Rockhampton and Toowoomba councils summarise them like this:

- **Road boundary:** typically 6 m on lots of 450 m² or more, and 3 to 4 m on smaller lots depending on the street.
- **Carport in the front setback:** an open carport can't go inside the 6 m road setback if there's another spot on the site that complies. Where there's no alternative location and no side access past the house, it can be allowed. If your design doesn't meet the code's setbacks, your certifier asks the council for a siting approval, called a referral or boundary relaxation.
- **Side and rear boundaries:** the standard clearance is 1.5 m, but a carport can be closer if it's no more than 4.5 m high, the total length of buildings within 1.5 m of that boundary is 9 m or less, and it's at least 1.5 m from a habitable room window of the neighbour's house.
- **Small lots:** on lots under 450 m², the width facing the street can't be more than 6 m or 50% of the frontage, whichever is less.
- **Site cover:** all roofed structures together can't exceed 50% of the lot.

Setbacks are measured to the outermost part of the carport, usually the gutter. Rockhampton also notes that a carport less than 450 mm from a side or rear boundary may need to be fire rated under the Building Code.

## NSW: exempt if it ticks every box

In NSW you can build a carport without planning or building approval if it meets all the exempt development standards in the State Environmental Planning Policy (Exempt and Complying Development Codes) 2008:

- **Floor area:** no more than 20 m² on lots of 300 m² or less; on larger lots, 25 m² in most zones or 50 m² in rural zones and zone R5.
- **Height:** no more than 3 m above existing ground, and not above the gutter line if attached to a single storey house.
- **Open sides:** at least 2 sides, and at least a third of the total perimeter, open.
- **Position:** at least 1 m behind the building line facing any road. The building line is the face of the wall, not a porch or portico.
- **Setbacks:** at least 900 mm from any lot boundary (5 m in zones RU1, RU2, RU3, RU4, RU6 and R5). The roof can project into that setback but must stay at least 500 mm from the boundary.
- **One per house:** one carport for each dwelling on the lot.
- **Other standards:** roof water to the existing stormwater system, any fascia connection to an engineer's specifications, low reflective pre-coloured metal, non-combustible materials on bushfire-prone land within 5 m of a house, and no loss of existing parking or vehicle access.

You can't build an exempt carport on a lot with a heritage item or in a foreshore area, and in a heritage conservation area it has to be in the rear yard. A new driveway or gutter crossing needs separate approval from the road authority, usually your council.

If your carport doesn't fit, it may still qualify as complying development, a combined planning and building approval from council or a private certifier. On residential lots that qualify, a detached carport can then be up to 4.5 m high, set back at least 5.5 m or 1 m behind the building line depending on the front setback.

## Victoria: always a building permit

In Victoria the answer is simple. Hume City Council states that construction of any garage, carport or verandah requires a building permit. Carports on or near a boundary also have to meet the siting rules in the Building Regulations 2018, and a design that doesn't can need the council's report and consent, with neighbours given a chance to comment. A registered building surveyor handles the permit and tells you which rules apply.

## WA: only the smallest freestanding carports

In WA, class 10 outbuildings such as carports, garages and sheds need no building permit only if they're **freestanding**, have a floor area of **10 m² or less**, are **no more than 2.4 m high within 1 m of a boundary** (3 m if 1 m or more away), aren't masonry, and sit **no closer to the street** than the house. A carport attached to the house, or anything over 10 m², needs a permit. Planning rules for front setbacks can apply too, so check with your council.

## What you can do yourself, and when to call a builder or certifier

Once approval is sorted, plenty of owners build a kit carport themselves: setting out, digging and concreting footings to the engineering, standing posts, and fitting beams and roof sheets with a helper.

Bring in a certifier or building surveyor before you buy when:

- it's over the exempt size, or you're in Victoria
- it's attached to the house or fixed to the fascia
- it's in the front setback or close to a side boundary
- you need a new driveway or gutter crossing
- your block has a heritage, bushfire, flood or easement issue

Ask the kit supplier for engineering drawings that match your wind region before you pay. Certifiers need them, and a carport roof is a big sail in a storm.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need council approval for a carport in QLD?**
Almost always. Only carports of 10 m² or less, no more than 2.4 m high and with no side over 5 m skip building approval, and Brisbane City Council says a carport on a property with an existing house needs building approval.

**Can I build a carport in front of my house?**
Not as exempt development in NSW, where it must be at least 1 m behind the building line. In Queensland it can be allowed in the front setback when there's no other spot on the site, usually through a siting approval. Elsewhere, ask your council.

**How close to the boundary can a carport be in NSW?**
For exempt development, 900 mm from any boundary in most zones, with the roof at least 500 mm from the boundary. In zones RU1 to RU4, RU6 and R5 it's 5 m.

**Does a carport need open sides?**
In NSW an exempt carport must be open on at least 2 sides and at least a third of its perimeter. Enclose it further and it no longer meets the exempt standard, so it needs approval.
