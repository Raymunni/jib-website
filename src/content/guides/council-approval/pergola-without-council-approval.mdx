A pergola over a typical back patio, say 6 x 4 m, is 24 m². In Queensland that's well over the no-approval limit. In NSW it's just inside it. Whether you add a roof matters just as much as the size.

## Limits by state

The short answer: size, height, roof and position all have to fit, and every state draws the lines differently.

| State | No approval needed if… |
|---|---|
| QLD | No more than 10 m² in plan area, 2.4 m high (mean height 2.1 m), no side longer than 5 m, and not in Wind Region C (tropical cyclone area) |
| NSW | Up to 25 m², no more than 3 m high, floor no more than 1 m above ground, behind the building line and at least 900 mm from boundaries (5 m in zones RU1 to RU4, RU6 and R5) |
| VIC | Unroofed, up to 20 m², no more than 3.6 m high, and no more than 2.5 m forward of the front wall of the house |
| WA | Associated with a house, no more than 2.4 m high, up to 20 m², and not in wind region C or D |
| SA, TAS, ACT, NT | Check with your council. We couldn't confirm their limits from an official source |

In every state, a heritage listing, bushfire or flood overlay, or an easement can cancel the exemption. Check your council's planning certificate or property report first.

## Queensland: 10 m² or you need a certifier

In Queensland a pergola is a class 10 structure, so it falls under schedule 1 of the Building Regulation 2021. It's "accepted" building work, with no building approval, only if:

- its plan area (including overhangs) is **no more than 10 m²**
- it's **no more than 2.4 m high**, with a mean height of no more than 2.1 m
- **no side is longer than 5 m**
- it's **not in Wind Region C**, the tropical cyclone area

A 3 x 3 m pergola (9 m²) can fit. A typical 6 x 4 m patio pergola (24 m²) can't, and needs building approval from a private building certifier before you start.

Even small pergolas still have to meet the siting rules in the Queensland Development Code. The usual side and rear clearance is 1.5 m, but Rockhampton Regional Council's summary of the code says an unroofed pergola can go right up to a side or rear boundary if it's not enclosed by walls, is no more than 2.4 m high at the boundary, is mainly ornamental or for plants, and the combined length of structures within the setback stays at 9 m or less. Roofed structures across the whole lot can't cover more than 50% of it.

Your council's planning scheme can still require planning approval, for example under a heritage or flood overlay. Business Queensland's guidance says to check with your council before starting, even for work that needs no building approval.

## NSW: exempt up to 25 m²

In NSW a pergola can be built as exempt development, with no planning or building approval, if it meets every standard in the State Environmental Planning Policy (Exempt and Complying Development Codes) 2008:

- **Area:** no more than 25 m².
- **Total of all such structures on the lot:** on lots over 300 m², no more than 15% of the ground floor area of the house; on lots of 300 m² or less, 25 m².
- **Height:** no more than 3 m above existing ground level at its highest point, with any floor no more than 1 m above ground.
- **Position:** behind the building line of any road frontage, and at least 900 mm from each lot boundary (5 m in zones RU1, RU2, RU3, RU4, RU6 and R5).
- **Walls:** any enclosing wall no higher than 1.4 m.
- **Bushfire-prone land:** within 5 m of a house, walls must be non-combustible.
- **Metal:** low reflective, factory pre-coloured materials.

A roofed pergola attached to the house has four extra rules: it can't extend above the roof gutter line, any fascia connection must follow an engineer's specifications, roof water must go into the existing stormwater system, and it can't interfere with existing drainage. If it can't meet all of these, NSW Planning says you need approval from your council before you build.

## Victoria: unroofed only

In Victoria a pergola is exempt from a building permit only if it's **unroofed**, has a floor area of **no more than 20 m²**, is **no more than 3.6 m high**, and sits **no more than 2.5 m forward of the front wall** of the house it's attached to. City of Monash explains that any roofing, even a partial roof, turns it into a verandah, which needs a building permit. If you plan to add shade cloth, confirm with your council's building team that it won't count as roofing before you buy.

## WA: 2.4 m high and 20 m²

In WA, a pergola associated with a house needs no building permit if it's **no more than 2.4 m high**, covers **no more than 20 m²**, and isn't in **wind region C or D**. Shade sails depend on the fabric: the Town of Victoria Park treats a sail with an impermeable cover such as vinyl as a verandah, which needs a permit, and one with permeable shade cloth as a pergola. Planning rules can still apply, so check with your council.

## Roof or no roof: why it changes everything

A solid roof is the single most common reason a "simple pergola" ends up needing approval. In Victoria and WA it changes the structure into a verandah. In NSW a roofed, attached pergola has to stay below your gutter line and drain to stormwater. In Queensland the size limit is the same either way, but a roof adds wind load, which is what certifiers check.

If you want shade without the paperwork, the realistic options are:

1. **An open pergola** within your state's limits, with a climbing plant or retractable shade cloth.
2. **A smaller pergola** that fits the exempt size, rather than one big structure.
3. **Getting approval up front** for the roofed patio you actually want. It's far easier than sorting out an unapproved one when you sell.

## What you can do yourself, and when to call a builder or certifier

You can usually build an exempt pergola yourself: setting out, digging post holes, concreting posts or fixing post anchors, and fitting beams and rafters. Follow the kit's engineering for your wind region and soil.

Call a building certifier (QLD), certifier or council (NSW) or building surveyor (VIC, WA) when:

- the pergola is over your state's exempt size or height
- you want a solid roof, especially attached to the house
- it needs to be fixed to the fascia or roof framing
- it's near a boundary and you're unsure of the clearance
- your property has a heritage, bushfire, flood or easement issue

If your pergola needs approval, the certifier or building surveyor will also tell you whether you can do the work yourself or need a licensed builder, so ask before you buy the kit.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need council approval for a pergola in QLD?**
Only if it's over 10 m², over 2.4 m high, has a side longer than 5 m, or is in a tropical cyclone area. Most patio pergolas are bigger than that and need building approval from a private certifier.

**Can I build a 3 x 3 m pergola without approval?**
Usually yes in NSW, Victoria (if unroofed) and WA. In Queensland, 9 m² is under the 10 m² limit, but it still has to be no more than 2.4 m high and outside a cyclone area.

**Is a shade sail a pergola?**
In WA, a sail with permeable shade cloth is treated as a pergola, while an impermeable cover such as vinyl makes it a verandah that needs a permit. In other states, ask your council. Sail posts are often tall, so check the height limit too.

**Can a pergola be built on the boundary?**
In NSW an exempt pergola must be at least 900 mm from every boundary. In Queensland an unroofed pergola can sit on a side or rear boundary if it's no more than 2.4 m high there and meets the other code conditions. Elsewhere, ask your council.
