Fence height rules split into two groups: **front fences** (facing the street), which are kept low for sight lines and streetscape, and **side and rear fences**, which can be taller for privacy.

## Limits by state

| State | Side and rear | Front / street frontage | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| NSW | 1.8 m (1.2 m if masonry) | 1.2 m | Secondary frontages must be partly see-through; not exempt on or next to a heritage item |
| VIC | 2 m | 1.5 m within 3 m of the street | 1 m within 9 m of a corner intersection; masonry front fences over 1.2 m need a permit |
| QLD | 2 m | — | Pool fences excluded |
| WA | 1.8 m (non-masonry, behind the front setback) | Needs a permit | Masonry and pool fences need a permit; councils add front-fence policies |
| SA | Check with your council | Check with your council | We couldn't confirm SA's limits from an official source |

### NSW corner blocks

In NSW, a fence along a secondary road frontage must be at least 20% see-through above 400 mm, with piers no wider than 350 mm. A solid 1.8 m fence is allowed along the rear half of the secondary frontage. On sloping ground, stepped fences can reach 1.5 m (front) or 2.2 m (side and rear) at the steps.

## Dividing fences: the neighbour side

Approval is only half of it. A fence on the boundary is also covered by dividing-fence laws, which decide who pays. In NSW, neighbours generally share the cost of a "sufficient" dividing fence equally under the Dividing Fences Act 1991, and anyone wanting something better pays the difference. Other states have similar laws with their own notice requirements.

- Talk to your neighbour before you start.
- Agree the height, material and cost split in writing.
- Confirm where the boundary actually is — an old fence isn't always on it.

## Before you build

- Check for heritage, bushfire and easement restrictions.
- Never use a boundary fence as a pool barrier without checking the pool-fencing rules.
- On bushfire-prone land in NSW, exempt fences must be non-combustible or hardwood.
