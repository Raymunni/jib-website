"You can build any shed under 10 square metres without approval" is repeated so often it sounds like law. It isn't — the limit is different in every state, and it's never the only condition.

## Limits by state

| State | Max floor area | Other key conditions |
|---|---|---|
| NSW | 20 m² (50 m² in rural zones RU1–4, RU6, R5) | No more than 3 m high; at least 900 mm from boundaries (5 m in those rural zones); behind the building line; at least 1 m from registered easements; not habitable; not a shipping container |
| VIC | Under 10 m² (freestanding) | Height and siting limits apply; a planning permit can still be needed under an overlay |
| QLD | 10 m² | Not in a tropical cyclone area; council siting and boundary-clearance rules still apply |
| WA | 10 m² | No more than 2.4 m high |
| SA | Check with your council | We couldn't confirm SA's limits from an official source |

## What still applies to an exempt shed

- **Setbacks and position:** even exempt sheds usually can't sit in front of the house or hard against the boundary.
- **Overlays:** heritage, bushfire, flood and easement restrictions can cancel the exemption.
- **Cyclone areas:** in Queensland, the 10 m² exemption doesn't apply in tropical cyclone areas, where wind loads are the whole point of approval.
- **Structure:** the shed still has to be built properly and tied down to suit your wind region — follow the kit's engineering, including the slab or footing details.
- **Use:** a garden shed can't be lived in. Turning it into a studio or granny flat is a different approval altogether.

## Before you buy the kit

1. **Measure your site** and mark the setbacks from each boundary.
2. **Check your council's planning certificate** or map for overlays.
3. **Check the kit's footprint** — some advertised sizes are roof dimensions, not floor area.
4. **Plan the base:** most kits need a concrete slab or a specific footing system. Check the kit's instructions for your wind region.
