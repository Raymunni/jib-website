Christmas brings more button batteries into the house than any other time of year: light-up decorations, musical cards, new remotes, toys, and the kitchen scale that comes out for the pudding. With young children or grandkids visiting, it's worth ten minutes to find them all.

## Why they're dangerous

ACCC Product Safety says a swallowed button battery can lodge in a child's throat, where saliva triggers a current that burns through tissue. **Serious injury can happen in as little as two hours**, and the damage can continue after the battery is removed. Batteries pushed into ears or noses also cause serious injuries.

Children **up to 5** are most at risk. Three children have died in Australia from button battery incidents.

## Where to look

The ACCC lists button batteries in:

- **Remote controls**, including the new ones from Christmas gifts
- **Musical greeting cards**
- **Toys** that light up, make noise or move
- **Kitchen and bathroom scales**, thermometers and other small medical devices
- **Watches, fitness devices, calculators, cameras and torches**

At Christmas, add **light-up decorations, LED tea lights and novelty gifts**: check what powers each one.

## The quick check

1. **Go room by room** and gather everything above.
2. **Check each battery compartment is secure** and child resistant, so a child can't get it open. Mandatory standards now cover how these products are designed and labelled, but the ACCC says to check even when buying from a seller you trust.
3. **Look for damage.** The ACCC says a battery can come loose if the product is broken or the compartment doesn't close properly. If so, stop using it and keep it away from children.
4. **Find the spares.** Store new and used batteries out of sight and reach, in a locked box or high cupboard.
5. **Deal with the dead ones** (below).

## Store and dispose of them safely

- Keep spares in their **child-resistant packaging**. Once opened, put them in a child-resistant container.
- **Change batteries on a flat surface,** away from children, so nothing rolls onto the floor.
- **Tape both sides of used batteries** straight away. The ACCC says this makes them harder to swallow and reduces the fire risk. A row of batteries can be taped together.
- **Recycle them** at a drop-off point. Don't leave them in a kitchen drawer.

## Buying gifts

- **Avoid button battery products** if you have young children or children visiting, where you can. The ACCC suggests alternatives that use other batteries, or rechargeables.
- **Check the compartment is secure** when the gift arrives, especially if bought online.
- **Look for warnings** on the packaging.
- **Check second-hand toys** for loose batteries and recalls.

## If you think a child has swallowed one

The ACCC's advice:

- **Call 000** if the child is bleeding or has any difficulty breathing.
- Otherwise call the **Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26** straight away, at any hour.
- **Don't wait for symptoms.** Don't let the child eat or drink, and don't make them vomit.

Signs can include gagging, drooling, coughing or noisy breathing, chest pain, vomiting, refusing food, or blood in vomit or stools. But children often can't tell you, so act on suspicion.

## Frequently asked questions

**What should I do if my child swallows a button battery?**
Call the Poisons Information Centre on 13 11 26 immediately, or 000 if they're bleeding or struggling to breathe. Don't wait for symptoms, and don't give food or drink.

**How do I dispose of button batteries safely?**
Tape both sides, keep them out of reach in a child-resistant container, and take them to a battery recycling drop-off.

**Which Christmas products have button batteries?**
Light-up decorations, musical cards, toys, remotes, tea lights and small gadgets often do.

**Are button batteries banned in Australia?**
No, but mandatory standards cover how they and the products containing them are designed, packaged and labelled.
