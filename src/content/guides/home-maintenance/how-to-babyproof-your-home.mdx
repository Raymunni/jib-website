Kidsafe says that where the place is known, almost half of children's injuries happen at home, and the youngest children are the most at risk. Babyproofing isn't about buying every gadget in the aisle. It's a short list of fixes for the things that kill or badly hurt young children, done before your baby can reach them, and then a slower sweep through the rest of the house.

Kidsafe's own method is the best place to start: **get down on your hands and knees** and go through each room at your baby's height. You'll see the cords, the cupboard handles and the gap behind the couch the way they will.

## When to start babyproofing

Babies get mobile faster than most parents expect, and often overnight. Aim to have the big fixes done **before your baby can roll**, at around four to six months, and the rest done before they crawl. Every baby is different, so watch what yours is doing rather than the calendar.

| Stage (roughly) | What they start doing | What to have done by then |
|---|---|---|
| Before the birth | Nothing yet, which is why it's the easiest time | Cot and mattress checked, hot water temperature, smoke alarms, safety switch, cords tied up, furniture anchored |
| Around 4 to 6 months | Rolling, grabbing, everything goes in the mouth | Change table habits, small objects off the floor, button batteries found, nothing on the couch or bed edge |
| Around 6 to 10 months | Crawling, sitting up, pulling on things | Gates on stairs and kitchen, cupboard latches on poisons, power point covers, heater and fire guards |
| Around 9 to 12 months | Pulling up to stand, cruising along furniture | Corner guards, tablecloths gone, TV strapped, drawers locked so they can't be climbed |
| 1 to 3 years | Walking, climbing, opening doors and child-resistant lids | Window locks, balcony furniture moved, pool gate checked, toilet lock, garage and shed locked |

## The hazards that matter most

If you only do part of this guide, do this table. These are the injuries that kill and badly hurt Australian children under five, according to Kidsafe, the ACCC and Royal Life Saving.

| Hazard | Why it's on the list | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Drowning** | The leading cause of death for children under five. Royal Life Saving counted 182 children aged 0 to 4 drowned in the 10 years to June 2025, with swimming pools the most common place. About three young children drown in a bath each year, more than half of them under one | A compliant pool fence with a self-closing gate, empty every bucket and paddling pool, and never leave a baby in the bath, even for a second |
| **Toppling furniture and TVs** | At least 28 deaths in Australia since 2000 and nearly 20 injuries a week. Children under five are most at risk | Anchor every tall or heavy piece to the wall and strap the TV |
| **Blind and curtain cords** | Young children have died in Australian homes after getting caught in a loop of cord | Keep cords 1.6m or more off the floor and move cots and beds away from windows |
| **Poisoning** | Kidsafe says 29 children a week are admitted to hospital for poisoning. Medicines and household cleaners are the most common causes | Lock them away, ideally 1.5m off the ground |
| **Burns and scalds** | Hot drinks and hot water are the usual cause, often with an adult right there | Hot drinks out of reach, bathroom water no hotter than 50°C, guards on heaters |
| **Falls** | The most common cause of injury to children, mostly head injuries and fractures | Gates on stairs, locks on windows, nothing to climb near a window or balcony |
| **Button batteries and magnets** | A swallowed button battery can cause serious burns inside within two hours | Secure battery compartments, spares locked away |
| **Driveways** | SA's transport department says about one child a week is run over in an Australian driveway, often a toddler | Fence the play area off from the driveway and know where your child is before you move the car |

## The first weekend: do these ten things

1. **Deal with every bit of water.** If you have a pool or spa, check the fence and gate (it must shut and latch on its own). Empty paddling pools straight after use and store them upright, put lids on nappy buckets, and never leave a baby or toddler alone in the bath.
2. **Anchor tall furniture and the TV.** Bookcases, chests of drawers, wardrobes, entertainment units, hall tables and buffets all go into the wall (how to do it is below). Strap the TV to the wall or to the unit, and anchor the unit too.
3. **Tie up blind and curtain cords.** Fit a cleat at least 1.6m off the floor or a tension device fixed to the wall or frame, and move the cot, beds, high chair and couch away from any corded window.
4. **Lock up medicines and chemicals.** Medicines, cleaners, dishwasher powder, laundry capsules, alcohol and garden chemicals go in a locked cupboard or box, preferably 1.5m up. Child-resistant lids slow children down, but Kidsafe points out they aren't childproof.
5. **Check the bathroom hot water.** Kidsafe says no hotter than 50°C at the bathroom tap. New and replaced systems must be set up that way; older houses can run much hotter. [How to check yours with a thermometer](/guides/home-maintenance/hot-water-temperature-50-degrees/).
6. **Stop windows opening wide.** Where a window has a drop of 2m or more below it, fit a lock or latch that stops it opening more than 12.5cm, or a fixed screen that can't be pushed out. Flyscreens won't stop a fall.
7. **Fit a screwed-in gate at the top of the stairs**, and a gate at the bottom as well.
8. **Find every button battery.** Remotes, car keys, scales, musical cards, light-up toys. Check each compartment closes properly and tape up used ones before you recycle them. [The full button battery check](/guides/home-maintenance/button-batteries-christmas-safety/).
9. **Check the cot.** It must meet the Australian standard (AS/NZS 2172, or AS/NZS 2195 for portable cots), with a firm mattress that fits snugly (Red Nose says no gap over 20mm), and nothing else in it.
10. **Test smoke alarms and the safety switch.** Press the test button on each. Kidsafe also says to have an electrical safety switch, which cuts the power when something goes wrong. [What to do if yours keeps tripping](/guides/how-to/why-does-my-safety-switch-keep-tripping/).

## Room by room

### Kitchen

- **Gate the doorway** if you can, so the kitchen is off limits while you cook.
- **Turn pot handles to the back** and use the back hotplates first. A stove guard stops little hands pulling a pot down.
- **Anchor a freestanding stove** to the floor or wall with its anti-tilt bracket. A child standing on an open oven door can tip it.
- **Cordless kettle, short cords**, and appliances pushed to the back of the bench so nothing dangles.
- **Placemats, not tablecloths.** A toddler pulling on a tablecloth brings everything on the table with it.
- **Never hold a hot drink with a child on your lap.** Kidsafe says boiled water can still scald for up to 30 minutes, so only boil what you need.
- **Latches on cupboards and drawers** with cleaners, dishwasher powder, knives, foil and plastic bags. If the cupboard under the sink is the only place for cleaners, latch it or move them up high.
- **Fire blanket and extinguisher** where an adult can reach them in a hurry.
- **Microwave out of reach**, or switched off at the wall if it sits low.

### Bathroom and laundry

- **Never leave a baby or toddler alone in the bath.** If you have to answer the door, take them with you. Bath seats and rings are not safety devices.
- **Empty the bath** as soon as you're done.
- **Cold water on first and off last** when you run a bath. Kidsafe recommends about 38°C for bathing young children, and a tap cover stops them turning on the hot.
- **Medicines, razors and cosmetics** in a locked cabinet, not on the vanity.
- **Hairdryers and straighteners unplugged** and put away. Straighteners stay hot long after they're off.
- **A lock on the toilet and the bathroom door** keeps toddlers away from the water.
- **Buckets have lids** and sit out of reach. Keep the washing machine and dryer doors shut.
- **A non-slip mat** in the bath or shower.

### Living room

- **Anchor the TV unit and bookcases**, strap the TV, and keep heavy things on the lowest shelves.
- **Don't put anything tempting on top** of a TV or a tall piece of furniture. Toys and remotes up there are what children climb for.
- **Corner guards** on coffee tables, benches and the fireplace hearth.
- **Cords tidied** behind furniture or bundled out of reach.
- **Fireplaces and heaters** get a guard fixed to the wall, not one that just stands there.
- **Rug grips** under loose rugs.
- **Check your plants.** Some common house plants are poisonous. Your local nursery can tell you which.
- **Handbags up high** when visitors come. They often carry medicines, lighters and button batteries.
- **Bean bags zipped closed.** The small beads can be breathed in.

### Nursery and bedrooms

- **Cot away from the window, cords, heaters and appliances**, in a clear space, with nothing a toddler could climb on beside it.
- **Baby on their back**, feet at the bottom of the cot, with head and face uncovered.
- **Nothing in the cot but the baby**: no bumpers, pillows, sleep positioners, extra padding or second mattress. Use the mattress size the cot was made for.
- **Cot bars 50 to 95mm apart**, per Kidsafe's checklist. Check second-hand cots for recalls and any missing parts before you use them.
- **Lower the mattress** as soon as your baby can sit up or pull to stand.
- **Change table: everything you need within reach**, and always a hand on the baby. Babies roll off raised surfaces fast.
- **Anchor the chest of drawers and the wardrobe**, and lock the drawers so they can't be used as steps.
- **A toy box without a hinged lid**, or one with a slow-closing hinge.
- **Bunk beds**: Kidsafe and the ACCC say the top bunk isn't for children under nine.
- **No electric blankets** for children under two or children who still wet the bed.
- **Low fire risk nightwear.** Look for the label.

### Stairs, halls and doors

- **Hardware-mounted gate at the top** of the stairs. It's screwed into the wall or a solid post. Gates that use pressure aren't secure enough there.
- **A gate at the bottom** too, so they can't climb up and fall.
- **Good lighting** on stairs, and a night light in the hall.
- **Finger guards on the hinge side** of doors children use, and door stoppers to stop slamming. Finger jams usually happen on the hinge side.
- **Glass doors and low windows**: older homes may not have safety glass. Fit safety film or a band of stickers at a child's eye level so nobody runs into it.
- **Front and back doors** that a toddler can't open, or a latch up high, especially if they lead to the driveway or the pool.

### Windows and balconies

- **Locks or latches that stop the window opening more than 12.5cm** wherever there's a drop of 2m or more below, or a fixed screen that can't be pushed out. The building code requires it for bedroom windows 2m or more above the ground in new homes. In NSW, strata buildings have needed window safety devices since March 2018.
- **Move beds, couches, toy boxes and chairs** away from windows. Children climb.
- **Balcony furniture and pot plants** go away from the railing, and the balcony door stays locked when nobody's out there.
- **Fill climbable gaps.** Rails with horizontal bars or gaps big enough to fit through need a screen or panel.

### Backyard, pool and garage

- **Pool and spa fence**: every pool or spa that can hold more than 30cm of water needs a child-resistant barrier, including portable and inflatable pools. Check the gate swings shut and latches from any position, and keep the area outside the fence clear of anything a child could climb. [NSW pool fence rules](/guides/home-maintenance/pool-fence-rules-nsw/) and [Queensland pool fence rules](/guides/home-maintenance/pool-fence-rules-qld/).
- **Stay within arm's reach** of children in or near water. Floaties aren't lifesaving devices.
- **Ponds get a fixed grill**, buckets get tipped out after rain, and post holes and trenches get covered.
- **Bird baths and statues cemented in** so they can't fall on a climbing child.
- **Shed and garage locked**, with chemicals, petrol, tools and pesticides in their original labelled containers.
- **Soft fall under play equipment**, and trampolines made to AS 4989 with the pads on.
- **The play area fenced off from the driveway.**

### The driveway

Kidsafe's rule for driveways is **supervise, separate, see**. Supervise children around cars, keep the driveway out of the play area with a fence or gate, and know exactly where your child is before you move the car, forwards or backwards. Don't let the driveway double as a play area, and hold their hand when cars are about.

## How to anchor furniture properly

The ACCC says the best way to stop furniture tipping is to fix it to the wall or floor. Most new furniture and TVs come with an anchor kit; if yours didn't, hardware stores sell them.

1. **Find a stud.** On a plasterboard wall, use a stud finder to find the timber or steel frame behind it, and screw the wall end of the strap into the stud. Plasterboard alone won't hold the weight of a falling chest of drawers.
2. **Brick, block or concrete walls** need masonry plugs or anchors sized for the screw.
3. **Fix the other end** to a solid part of the furniture, usually the top rail at the back, not a thin back panel.
4. **Strap the TV** with the straps that came with it, to the wall or to the unit. If it's strapped to the unit, anchor the unit too. A wall-mounted TV is safer again.
5. **Put heavy things in the bottom drawers** and on low shelves, and fit drawer locks so the drawers can't be pulled out as steps. Furniture on carpet or a rug is less stable.
6. **Check them once a year.** Straps loosen and furniture gets moved.

## Which gate goes where

The ACCC says safety gate bars should be **5 to 9.5cm apart**. There's no mandatory standard for safety gates in Australia, so check before you buy.

| Where | Gate type | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Top of the stairs | Hardware-mounted, screwed in | A fall from the top is the worst case, and pressure gates can be pushed out. Avoid a bar across the bottom that someone could trip on |
| Bottom of the stairs | Either type | Stops them climbing up |
| Kitchen or hallway doorway | Pressure-mounted is fine on a solid timber frame | Quick to fit and take down |
| Fireplace, heater or kitchen with no door | Hardware-mounted guard fixed to the wall | It has to hold when a toddler leans on it |

Skip the diamond-pattern accordion gates, which can trap heads and clothing, and don't use pet gates for children. Choose a gate you can open with one hand while you carry the baby, and check it's still tight every few weeks.

## What to skip

- **Baby walkers.** Kidsafe doesn't recommend them, and the ACCC says over 100 children go to emergency each year because of them, mostly from falls down stairs.
- **Cot bumpers, pillows and sleep positioners.** Red Nose and the ACCC say keep them out of the cot.
- **A pressure-mounted gate at the top of the stairs.**
- **Flyscreens as fall protection.** A child can push straight through one.
- **Water beads.** The ACCC has warned they can cause serious injury if swallowed, and says to keep them away from young children.
- **Relying on gadgets instead of supervision.** Kidsafe's rule is to remove the hazard if you can, guard it if you can't, and watch children closely either way.

## Babyproofing a rental

You can do a lot without touching the walls: pressure-mounted gates, adhesive cupboard latches, power point covers, corner guards and moving furniture away from windows. For anything that needs a screw, the rules depend on your state.

| State | What the rules say |
|---|---|
| **NSW** | A landlord can't unreasonably refuse minor changes, including securing furniture to a wall (other than a tiled wall), installing child safety gates indoors, window safety devices, cleats or cord guides for blind cords, and shatter-resistant film. You still need the landlord's written consent first. |
| **Victoria** | You can fit pressure-mounted gates and adhesive locks on drawers and doors without asking. Unless the property is heritage listed, you can also fit wall anchors for furniture, blind or cord anchors and hardware-mounted gates, except on exposed brick or concrete walls. For those walls you ask first, and the landlord needs a good reason to say no. |
| **WA** | You ask to anchor furniture using Consumer Protection's request form, and the landlord must agree unless it would disturb asbestos, the property is heritage listed or a strata by-law prohibits it. Landlords must provide safety devices for loose curtain cords. |
| **Other states and territories** | Ask the landlord in writing, explain it's for child safety, and offer to remove the fixings and patch the holes when you move out. |

In every state, plan to remove what you added and repair the holes when you leave, unless the landlord agrees otherwise.

## Visiting family and holidays

Grandparents' houses and holiday rentals are rarely babyproofed. Kidsafe's tips:

- **Ask about water first.** Many children have drowned at friends' homes because their parents didn't know there was a pool, spa, pond or dam.
- **Check bedrooms and bathrooms for medicines** within reach, and find a high spot for visitors' handbags.
- **Ask if there's a place to play away from the dog.**
- **Look up.** Children have been hurt by being lifted into ceiling fan blades.
- **Pack a few portable fixes**: adhesive latches, power point covers and a travel cot that meets AS/NZS 2195.

## First aid to know before you need it

Put these on the fridge:

- **Emergency: 000.**
- **Poisons Information Centre: 13 11 26**, any hour, Australia wide. Take the container to the phone with you.
- **Burns and scalds:** cool running water for at least 20 minutes, take off clothing and jewellery if you can, then get medical advice. No ice, butter or creams.
- **Button battery swallowed or put in an ear or nose:** call 13 11 26 straight away, or 000 if they're struggling to breathe. Don't wait for symptoms.
- **Choking baby under one:** call 000 straight away and follow the St John DRSABCD action plan.

A first aid course that covers CPR for babies and children is the single best thing to add to this list.

## When can you stop?

Some of it never comes off. **Furniture anchors, cord cleats, the pool fence and locked-away medicines** stay for good, and they protect visiting children too. Gates come down when your child can open or climb them, because a gate they can climb is a fall risk of its own; check the label for the age it's made for. Cupboard latches and drawer locks can go once your child understands the rules, but keep poisons locked away regardless.

## Frequently asked questions

**When should I start babyproofing my house?**
Before your baby can roll, at around four to six months, and ideally before the birth for the big things like anchoring furniture, tying up blind cords, the cot and hot water. Babies can go from not moving to crawling in a few days.

**What is the most important thing to babyproof?**
Water. Drowning is the leading cause of death for Australian children under five. After that, anchoring furniture and TVs, blind cords, poisons, hot water and stairs.

**Do I need a baby gate at the top and bottom of the stairs?**
Kidsafe says use gates or barriers at both. The gate at the top should be screwed into the wall or a solid post. Pressure-mounted gates aren't secure enough there.

**Are pressure-mounted baby gates safe?**
For doorways with a solid timber frame, yes. The ACCC says they're weaker than screwed-in gates, so don't use one at the top of the stairs or in front of a fireplace.

**What furniture needs to be anchored?**
Anything a child could pull over or climb: bookcases, chests of drawers, wardrobes, cabinets, entertainment units, hall tables, buffets and sideboards, plus the TV. Children under five are most at risk.

**How do I anchor furniture without drilling?**
Not reliably. Adhesive and no-drill anchors aren't a substitute for a strap screwed into a stud or masonry. If you rent, ask the landlord in writing. In NSW, Victoria and WA the law makes it hard for a landlord to say no.

**How do I make blind cords safe?**
Fit a cleat at least 1.6m above the floor and wrap the cord around it, or a tension device fixed to the wall or window frame so the cord can't form a loop. Move cots, beds, couches and anything climbable away from the window. Cordless blinds remove the risk.

**Do I need power point covers in Australia?**
Kidsafe recommends plug-in covers in unused power points, and some newer power points have built-in shutters. Covers stop children poking things in, but the real protection is a working safety switch, which cuts the power if something goes wrong.

**Do inflatable pools need a fence?**
In NSW, Queensland and WA, any portable or inflatable pool that can hold more than 30cm of water needs a pool fence by law. Smaller ones should be emptied straight after use and stored upright.

**Are baby walkers safe?**
Kidsafe doesn't recommend them. Babies in walkers can reach heaters and hot drinks and roll down stairs, and the ACCC says over 100 children a year go to emergency because of them.

**How do I babyproof a fireplace?**
Fit a fireguard fixed to the wall around the fireplace or heater, pad the hearth edges with corner guards or a cushion strip, and keep matches, lighters and fire tools out of reach.

**How do I babyproof a TV unit?**
Strap the TV to the wall or the unit, anchor the unit to the wall, latch any doors or drawers, and keep remotes and toys off the top so there's nothing to climb for.

**Do I need to babyproof the toilet?**
Kidsafe's checklist suggests a lock on the toilet and the bathroom door. A toddler can drown in very little water, so keeping them out of the bathroom is the simplest fix.

**Do I need to babyproof a rental?**
Yes, the risks are the same. Use the fixes that don't need screws first, and ask the landlord in writing for anchors, gates and window locks. In NSW, Victoria and WA there are rules that help renters with these.

**When can baby gates come down?**
When your child can open them or climb them, because a climbed gate becomes a fall risk. Check the label for the age the gate is made for. Keep the pool fence, anchors and locked-away medicines for good.

**Is babyproofing really necessary?**
The basics are. Kidsafe says around 150 Australian children die from unintentional injuries each year, and the youngest are most at risk at home. You don't need every product, but anchoring, water, cords, poisons, hot water and stairs are worth doing in every home.
