Hot tap water seriously scalds small children, older people and people with disabilities every year in Australia, and Victoria's plumbing regulator says more than 90% of those scalds happen in the bathroom. The fix is a rule most people have never heard of: water at the bathroom taps should come out at 50°C or less.

Here's how the rule works, why your tank runs hotter than that, and how to check your own taps.

## The rules at a glance

| Where | Maximum at the outlet |
|---|---|
| Showers, baths, basins and bidets in a home | **50°C** |
| Aged care, health care patient areas, childcare, schools and disability facilities | **45°C** |
| The water stored in the tank | Kept **above 60°C** (to stop bacteria such as Legionella) |

The 50°C limit applies to fixtures used for personal hygiene. That's why your kitchen sink and laundry tub can still run hotter.

## Why 50°C, and not 60°C?

The Victorian Building and Plumbing Commission puts it simply:

- At **68°C**, a full thickness scald can take as little as **one second**.
- At **50°C**, it takes **five minutes**.

That gap is the difference between a split second a toddler can't react to, and time for anyone to pull away. 50°C is still plenty for a shower. It isn't a bathing temperature, though: the recommended maximum bath water for young children is **37 to 38°C**, so you still mix in cold.

## Why the tank stays at 60°C or more

Stored hot water has to stay above 60°C to stop bacteria growing, including Legionella. So you can't just turn the whole system down. Instead, a device near the heater mixes cold water into the hot on its way to the bathroom:

- **Tempering valve:** the common one in homes. It brings bathroom hot water down to 50°C and leaves the kitchen and laundry hotter.
- **Thermostatic mixing valve:** a more precise valve that holds a set temperature. Required where the 45°C limit applies.
- **Thermostatically controlled tap** or a **temperature-limited water heater** marked for 50°C delivery.
- **Continuous flow (instantaneous) systems:** you set the delivery temperature on the control pad.

## Does your house have one?

Victoria's regulator says systems installed before **5 August 1998** are unlikely to be tempered.

The current NSW advisory note spells out when a temperature control device is needed today:

| Work done | Device needed? |
|---|---|
| Installing a hot water system for the first time | Yes |
| Replacing the heater, like for like or a different type | Yes |
| Moving the heater to a new spot | Yes |
| Replacing a whole bathroom's fixtures (renovation) | Yes, for those fixtures |
| Replacing a valve, element or thermostat on the heater | No |
| Replacing a single basin tap | No |
| Replacing solar collector panels only | No |

So if your hot water system is older than your last bathroom reno, or older than 1998, it's worth checking.

## How to check your bathroom tap

**You need:** a kitchen or cooking thermometer and a glass.

1. Pick the bathroom basin or the bath, not the kitchen.
2. Turn the tap to **full hot only** and let it run for a minute or two until the temperature stops climbing.
3. Fill the glass and put the thermometer straight in. Read it after 20 to 30 seconds.
4. Check the shower too if it's on a separate tap or mixer.

This is a quick home check, not a compliance test. But if the bathroom reads well over 50°C, or the temperature has changed suddenly, get a plumber to look at it.

## Signs the tempering valve needs a plumber

- The bathroom hot water is suddenly much hotter, or barely warm, while the kitchen is fine.
- The temperature surges up and down during a shower.
- The thermometer check shows well over 50°C.
- You have no idea whether there's a valve at all, and the system predates 1998 or your bathroom reno.

Tempering valves don't last forever, so check the manufacturer's instructions on the valve or with the hot water system for how often it should be serviced.

## DIY or a plumber?

- **DIY:** the thermometer check, running cold first, keeping kids supervised in the bath, setting the temperature on a continuous flow control pad.
- **Licensed plumber:** adjusting, servicing or replacing a tempering valve or thermostatic mixing valve, and any work on the hot water system itself. In NSW any plumbing work needs a licence regardless of cost, and the owner repairs allowed for taps specifically exclude thermostatic mixing and tempering valves.

## If someone is scalded

1. Remove clothing quickly, unless it's stuck to the skin.
2. Cool the burn under **cold running water for 20 minutes**. No butter, oil or creams.
3. Cover it loosely with a non-stick dressing, cling film or a clean cloth and keep the person warm.
4. See a doctor if the scald is bigger than 3cm, blistered, or on the hands, face or genitals. Call 000 for anything serious.

## Frequently asked questions

**What temperature should hot water be at the tap in Australia?**
No more than 50°C at bathroom fixtures in homes with a new or replaced system, and 45°C in childcare, aged care, schools and similar places.

**Can I turn my hot water system down to 50°C?**
Not on a storage system. Stored water must stay above 60°C to stop bacteria, so the temperature is lowered at the outlet with a tempering valve instead. Continuous flow systems can be set on the control pad.

**Can I adjust my own tempering valve?**
No. Adjusting or replacing a tempering valve is licensed plumbing work.

**Why is my kitchen tap hotter than the shower?**
That's normal. The 50°C limit only covers bathroom fixtures, so a tempering valve usually serves the bathroom while the kitchen and laundry get water at full temperature.
