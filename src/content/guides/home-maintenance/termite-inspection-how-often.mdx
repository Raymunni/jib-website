Termites do their damage out of sight. By the time a door sticks or a skirting board crumbles, they may have been in the house for a long time, which is why a yearly inspection is such cheap insurance against an expensive surprise. Here's how often to check, what to look for yourself, and the one thing you shouldn't do if you find them.

## How often to get an inspection

**At least once a year.** The QBCC says to have a licensed pest controller inspect and report on your property every year, or more often in high-hazard areas. The Victorian Building Authority and CHOICE give similar advice.

Consider going more often if:

- You're in a warm, humid area, or your pest controller says the local risk is high
- You've had termites before, here or next door
- You have a lot of timber close to the ground, such as a low deck or sleeper garden beds
- Your house has a timber frame or subfloor, or a damp area underneath

Ask your pest controller what suits your place, and keep every written report. It's useful for the next inspection, and for any future buyer.

## What to look for yourself

You won't see most of the activity, because it usually happens in concealed spaces. Still, a walk around every few months is worthwhile. Older Queensland DPI guidance suggests owner checks every three to four months.

- **Mud tubes:** thin tunnels of dried mud running up stumps, piers, slabs or walls.
- **Hollow timber:** tap skirting boards, door frames and window frames with a screwdriver handle. Damaged timber can sound hollow or feel soft.
- **Sticking doors and windows**, or floors that have started to sag.
- **Paint that bubbles or looks like water damage** where there hasn't been any water.
- **Damp spots:** leaking taps, hot water relief lines and air conditioner drains near the house.
- **Bridges over the barrier:** garden beds, paving or stored timber against the house.

Stay safe under the house. If you can't get in safely, leave it for the inspector.

## Found termites? Don't touch them

This is the mistake most people make, and it's an easy one to make. **Don't spray them, break open the tubes, or poke around in the timber.** Queensland DPI advises that workings shouldn't be disturbed until a management approach has been decided, because a disturbed colony may move elsewhere and be harder to treat.

1. **Leave everything as it is.**
2. **Photograph what you can see**, and note where it is.
3. **Call a licensed pest controller** for an inspection and written report.
4. **Get it fixed before repairing the timber.** Treat first, then repair, and get a builder to check anything structural.

## Everyday jobs that invite termites in

Your home may have a termite barrier, and it may be doing its job. These common jobs can quietly work against it. The QBCC warns that footpaths, garden beds, patios, pergolas and landscaping, and even laying turf, may make a termite system less effective.

| Everyday habit | The problem | The fix |
|---|---|---|
| Garden beds or mulch against the slab | Covers the slab edge, where you'd spot mud tubes | Keep the edge visible and clear |
| Firewood or timber stored against the house | Food, and a bridge to the house | Store it away from the house and off the ground |
| Leaking taps, or drains running by the wall | Termites are attracted to damp | Fix leaks and drainage |
| Old stumps and offcuts left in the garden | Food supply close to home | Remove them |
| Sleepers and timber touching the ground | Direct path for termites | Use treated timber, and check it regularly |
| Paving or new work over a barrier | May bridge or damage it | Check with your pest controller first |

If you've just added a deck, a garden bed or new paving, mention it at your next inspection.

## Who can do termite work?

Inspections and treatments should be done by a licensed pest controller. Check their licence with your state's regulator and ask for a written report. The Australian standard for termite management is AS 3660, and new buildings in many areas must have termite risk management under the National Construction Code. That doesn't mean a newer home is immune: barriers need to stay intact and be maintained.

Rules for who can use termite chemicals are strict, so a shop-bought spray isn't a substitute for a treatment. And since termite damage often isn't covered by home insurance, check your own policy, because a yearly report is much easier to live with than a surprise repair bill.

## Buying a house?

Get a separate timber pest inspection before you buy. A building inspection alone doesn't always cover it, so ask exactly what's included. AS 4349.3 is the standard for timber pest inspections.

## Frequently asked questions

**How often should you have a termite inspection?**
At least once a year, and more often in high-risk areas, as the QBCC advises. Ask your pest controller what suits your home.

**What are the first signs of termites?**
Often mud tubes on stumps or walls, hollow-sounding timber, sticking doors and bubbling paint. Many homes show no obvious signs until an inspection finds them.

**What should I do if I find termites?**
Don't disturb them or spray them. Photograph what you see and call a licensed pest controller.

**Do termites only attack old houses?**
No. Any home with timber in it can be at risk, including those on concrete slabs, where termites can come up around pipes and edges.

**Does home insurance cover termite damage?**
Termite damage usually isn't covered, but policies vary, so read yours.
